# Mechanical

## What the consultant issues

- Ductwork layout plans, coordinated with the reflected ceiling plan
- Plant schedules — units, capacities, electrical loads, weights
- Controls and BMS points schedule
- Sections through congested ceiling zones
- Commissioning data and an air balance report at completion

Sheet prefix is normally **M**. Governing standards: AS 1668.2 for ventilation rates, AS 1668.1 where
air handling crosses fire-rated construction.

## Where it bites on a retail fitout

**Ceiling void congestion.** The ceiling void is the most contested space in a fitout. Ductwork,
sprinkler pipework, cable tray, lighting, structure and the ceiling itself all want the same 400mm.
Mechanical usually wins on size and loses on flexibility, so it should be set out first and everything
else coordinated around it.

**Ventilation rate follows use, not area.** A change of use — adding a coffee offer, converting back
of house to a staff room, increasing occupancy — changes the required outdoor air. A tenancy fitout
drawn for one occupancy and built for another is a real compliance gap, and it usually surfaces at
commissioning when it is expensive.

**Landlord plant capacity.** Most retail tenancies rely on base building chilled water, condenser
water or a supply air allowance. The lease will state an allowance. **Check the design against the
allowance, not against the space.** Exceeding it means either a landlord approval or supplementary
plant, and both take time.

**Kitchen exhaust.** Any food offer triggers exhaust, make-up air, and usually a fire-rated duct
riser to roof. NCC 2025 broadened the kitchen exhaust hood provisions (F6D6, F6D12). This is the
single most common item to be under-scoped at tender.

**Fire mode.** Where ductwork crosses a fire-rated element, the opening protection has to be right —
fire dampers, smoke dampers, or a tested penetration system. NCC 2025 aligned the terminology with
AS 1668.1 and added smoke damper requirements in several places.

## Review checklist

- Is there a stated outdoor air rate, and does it match the occupancy the architectural set shows?
- Does the design load sit inside the landlord's stated plant allowance? Is that allowance quoted
  anywhere in the set?
- Is every penetration of a fire-rated element shown with its damper or seal type?
- Are supply and return positions coordinated with the reflected ceiling plan, or drawn independently
  of it? Independently-drawn RCP and duct layouts are a guaranteed clash.
- Is there a section through the tightest ceiling zone? If not, the coordination has not been tested.
- Do plant weights appear anywhere the structural engineer would see them?
- Is condensate drainage shown, and where does it discharge to?
- Are controls and BMS integration with the base building described, or left as "by others"?

## Common coordination clashes

| Clash | How it shows up |
|---|---|
| Duct versus structure | Duct routed through a downstand beam zone. Only visible in section. |
| Duct versus sprinkler main | Both at high level on the same axis, neither drawn with the other |
| Diffuser versus light fitting | RCP and mechanical plan disagree on the ceiling grid module |
| Exhaust versus tenancy boundary | Kitchen exhaust riser needs a route to roof that crosses another tenancy or a common area |
| Condensate versus hydraulic | Condensate drain with nowhere compliant to discharge |

## Interface with the tenancy

Base building provides the capacity; the tenant provides everything downstream of the isolation
valve or the tenancy connection point. **Where that point is, is a lease question, and it is worth
resolving on paper before tender** — an assumption that the landlord's plant reaches further into the
tenancy than it does is a common source of variation.
