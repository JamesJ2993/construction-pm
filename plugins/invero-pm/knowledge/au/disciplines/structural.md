# Structural

## What the consultant issues

- General arrangement plans, usually one per level
- Sections and details
- Slab penetration and setout plans
- Connection details, often as a typical-details sheet
- A structural certificate or design compliance statement at completion

Sheet prefix is normally **S** (S001, S101…). On a fitout the structural package is often thin —
a few sheets — because the work is limited to penetrations, new loads and localised framing.

## Where it bites on a retail fitout

**Penetrations.** Every core hole, every duct opening, every hanger fixed into the slab is a
structural question. The two failure modes:

1. **Post-tensioned slabs.** Cutting a tendon is a structural failure, not a defect. Scanning before
   drilling is mandatory, not prudent. If the base building drawings do not say whether the slab is
   post-tensioned, that is a clarification, not an assumption.
2. **Cumulative penetrations.** Individually approved holes that collectively remove more than the
   engineer allowed. Nobody tracks this unless the PM does.

**New loads.** Plant on the roof or slab, a mezzanine, heavy stock storage, a safe, a feature ceiling
carrying more than plasterboard. The question is always the same: what is the existing capacity, and
who has confirmed it? A design that shows the load without a capacity check is incomplete.

**Shopfront framing.** Portal frames and head supports at the lease line, and how they connect back
to base building structure. This is a common interface fight because the shopfront is tenant work but
the thing it fixes to is landlord property.

**Existing condition.** On a refit, what is actually there rarely matches the base building drawings.
A dilapidation survey before demolition protects against inheriting someone else's damage.

## Review checklist

- Is the slab type stated? Post-tensioned, conventionally reinforced, composite, suspended?
- Are penetrations dimensioned and located, or shown indicatively? Indicative penetrations are a
  clarification.
- Do the penetration sizes on the structural sheets match the mechanical, hydraulic and electrical
  sets? Cross-discipline penetration mismatch is one of the most reliable findings on any set.
- Is there a stated allowable imposed load, and does the proposed use stay under it?
- Are new fixings specified with a substrate? "Fix to slab" is not a specification.
- Is a scanning requirement noted before coring?
- For a mezzanine: egress from it, fire rating of it, and its load path — all three, not just the
  frame.

## Common coordination clashes

| Clash | How it shows up |
|---|---|
| Duct versus beam | Mechanical routes a duct through a zone the structural sheets show as a downstand. Check the section, not the plan. |
| Penetration versus tendon | Hydraulic shows a floor waste where the structural setout shows a tendon band |
| Hanger versus service | Ceiling hangers landing in the same zone as a sprinkler main |
| Shopfront fixing versus base building | Tenant frame fixed into a landlord element the lease does not permit modifying |

A finding here needs a measured clearance, not a suspicion. "Possible clash" is not a register entry;
"duct soffit at 2,750 AFFL versus beam soffit at 2,690 AFFL, 60mm short" is.

## Interface with the tenancy

The structural engineer is usually engaged by the tenant for fitout work but is constrained by base
building information they did not produce and cannot verify. Where base building structural
information is missing or contradictory, that is a landlord request, and it is on the critical path
far more often than it looks — a structural certificate can hold up an occupancy certificate.
