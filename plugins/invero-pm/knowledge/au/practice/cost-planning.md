# Preliminaries — price the org structure that actually turns up

The most common error in assessing a shop fitter's preliminaries is pricing them as though a head
contractor's organisation were on site. It is not, and the difference is large enough to swamp every
other line in a tender comparison.

## The benchmark

For a CBD retail fitout, direct time-related preliminaries run at roughly **$6,500 per week**, or
about **7–9% of construction value**.

For contrast, the same scope priced against the wrong structure:

| Priced as | Rough weekly prelims |
|---|---|
| **Shop fitter** — the correct basis | **~$6,500** |
| Builder | ~$10,000 |
| Head contractor | ~$14,000+ |

This benchmark came from a retail fitout where the submitted preliminaries were overstated by roughly half. The gap was almost entirely org-structure assumptions, not rates.

## Why a shop fitter's prelims are lower

- **The working foreman is genuinely working.** They are on the tools for much of the week, so only
  around **60%** of their time belongs in preliminaries. Pricing a full-time non-productive foreman is
  the single biggest overstatement.
- **The PM runs five or six stores at once.** On any one store that is roughly **0.15–0.25 FTE**, not
  a full-time allocation.
- **There is no site-based contract administrator.** That role sits in the office, across the whole
  programme.
- **Trades carry their own waste removal, EWPs and small plant.** A head contractor centralises these
  into prelims; a shop fitter does not, and pricing them centrally double-counts what is already in
  the trade rates.

## Two traps

**Do not add an overtime loading for salaried staff on a 6- or 7-day programme.** Salaried supervision
does not attract penalty rates. The loading gets added by analogy with hourly trades and is simply
wrong.

**A compressed programme reduces preliminaries, it does not increase them.** Time-related costs are
time-related. A shorter programme means fewer weeks of them. Compression may increase *trade* costs
through overtime and additional labour, but the prelims line should fall. A tender that shows
preliminaries rising with compression has not been built from first principles.

## The consequence for delay claims

Lean preliminaries are correct for pricing the work, but they make a **weak prolongation rate**. A
contractor delayed on a job with $6,500/week prelims will recover $6,500/week, which will not cover
what a genuine delay actually costs them.

Where delay risk is real, recommend a **separate, higher delay-costs rate** agreed at contract stage
rather than relying on the prelims rate as a proxy. It is fairer to both sides and it removes an
argument later.

## How to use this in a tender analysis

1. Ask for the preliminaries build-up, not just the total. A total cannot be interrogated.
2. Check each role against the org structure that will actually attend, and against the allocation
   that role really has across the contractor's portfolio.
3. Check what is already in the trade rates before accepting it in prelims — waste, EWPs, small
   plant, scaffold.
4. Check the weeks against the programme, and check that a compressed programme has reduced them.
5. Benchmark the weekly rate and the percentage of construction value against the figures above.
   Both should land in range; if either is well outside, the build-up will explain why, or it will
   not stand up.

A preliminaries assessment that names the specific over-allocations is worth far more than one that
says the number looks high — and it is much harder to argue with.
