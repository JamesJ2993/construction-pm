# The base building interface

Most fitout disputes are not design disputes. They are boundary disputes — an argument about where
landlord work stops and tenant work starts, conducted in the language of drawings.

The boundary is set by three documents that rarely agree:

1. **The lease** — the legal position, including the fitout guide and any base building scope annexed
   to it
2. **The base building drawings** — what was built, or at least what was drawn
3. **The tenancy design** — what the tenant's consultants assumed

**When these three disagree, the lease wins legally and the site wins physically.** The drawings win
nothing. Verify on site before tender.

## The connection points to nail down before tender

For each, the questions are the same: where exactly is it, what capacity does it have, and who pays
to extend it?

| Service | The question that costs money |
|---|---|
| Electrical | Where is the tenancy isolator, and what is the supply allowance in amps or kVA? Long submains grow for voltage drop, not current. |
| Mechanical | Is it chilled water, condenser water, or a supply air allowance — and how much? Where does the landlord's provision physically stop? |
| Hydraulic | Where is the capped water point and the drainage connection, and at what invert? An invert that does not work forces a step, a pump, or a moved fixture. |
| Fire | Is the landlord's fire contractor mandated? A mandated trade removes competitive pricing and must be priced accordingly. |
| Data | Where is the carrier lead-in, and what is its lead time? Long, and nobody's problem until it is. |
| Grease / trade waste | Is there a route and space for an arrestor, and has the water authority application started? |

## Cold shell, warm shell, and what was actually delivered

A "cold shell" handover means the tenant gets bare structure and services capped at a boundary. A
"warm shell" means some base building work — often ceilings, sprinklers to a base grid, and an air
supply — is already in.

**What the lease calls the shell and what is physically there are frequently different.** The base
building scope of work document is the thing to read, not the label. Then walk the site against it.
Items shown as landlord work but not delivered become variations if the tenant builds them, and
disputes if the tenant does not.

## Make good

Read the make good obligation at the *start* of the job, not the end. Two things matter:

- **What state the tenancy must be returned to.** Original condition, base building condition, or as
  varied by the landlord's consent. These are materially different and the difference is a provision
  on the client's balance sheet.
- **What the landlord consented to.** Alterations the landlord approved in writing are usually
  exempt from make good; alterations made without consent are not. **Keep the consents.** They are
  worth money at the end of the term and they are the documents most likely to be lost.

## Landlord consent and the approvals it gates

Landlord consent is a separate approval from any statutory one, and it usually has to come first —
a certifier will often want evidence of the owner's consent before issuing. Building it into the
programme as a predecessor rather than a parallel activity is the difference between a realistic
programme and an optimistic one.

Consent typically covers: the design, any structural alteration, any change to a base building
service, signage, and the contractors used where trades are mandated.

## Access, hours and the things that quietly wreck a programme

Shopping centre tenancies have constraints a standalone site does not:

- **Trading hours.** Noisy or disruptive work restricted to after hours, which is a productivity and
  cost multiplier, not just an inconvenience.
- **Loading dock booking.** Limited windows, shared with every other tenancy and the centre's own
  logistics.
- **Lift and hoist access.** Booked, sized, and often the constraint on material sizes.
- **Induction.** Every worker, before they start, at the centre's convenience.
- **Hoarding and protection.** Landlord-specified, often to a standard the tenant has not seen at
  tender.

Each of these belongs in the preliminaries and in the programme. Costing a centre fitout as if it
were a standalone site is the most common estimating error, and it does not show up until site
starts.

## What to raise on a register

Anything where the drawing set assumes a base building condition it has not verified:

- A connection point shown without a source document or a site verification
- "By landlord" on a drawing without a corresponding line in the base building scope
- A capacity relied on but not quoted — supply allowance, plant allowance, drainage invert
- Base building information that is missing, contradictory, or superseded
- Landlord consent assumed rather than held

Each of those is a real risk carried by the client until someone resolves it. Naming it on a register
moves it from an assumption to a tracked item, which is most of the value a PM adds at this stage.
